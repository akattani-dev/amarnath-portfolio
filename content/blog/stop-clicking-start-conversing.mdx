Every enterprise platform I have worked on has the same hidden layer: a set of
runbooks. Create the private space. Check the CIDR does not overlap. Confirm the
environment exists. Attach the transit gateway. Wait for the status to flip.

None of that lives in the platform. It lives in a Confluence page, a senior
engineer's head, and the muscle memory of whoever did it last quarter.

## The console is the bottleneck, not the API

The APIs have been there for years. What has not existed is anything that knows
the *order*, the *pre-checks*, and the *point at which a human should be asked*.
So we kept a person in the loop for the whole sequence — not because judgement
was needed at every step, but because there was nowhere else to put the
knowledge.

That is the part agents actually change. Not speed. Sequencing.

## What a good platform agent looks like

The version that works in a real enterprise is boring on purpose:

1. **Route the request** to a domain — networking, access management, lifecycle.
2. **Collect inputs one at a time**, validated against real constraints.
3. **Run read-only pre-checks** before proposing anything.
4. **Show a dry run** — the exact call, the exact arguments.
5. **Pause for explicit approval** on anything destructive.
6. **Execute**, then verify.

Notice how little of that is model work. The model reasons; deterministic
guidance supplies the steps, the constraints and the approval gates. When people
say agents are unreliable in production, they usually mean they handed the model
the whole workflow and hoped.

> The guidance layer is what makes the agent auditable. The model is what makes
> it usable.

## Where it gets uncomfortable

Two things break the illusion quickly.

The first is **blast radius**. A human clicking through a console is slow, and
slowness is an accidental safety feature. Remove it and you need real gates —
not a confirmation dialog the agent can answer itself.

The second is **observability**. If an agent performs six platform calls to
satisfy one request, the audit trail has to reconstruct intent, not just the
calls. "Who deleted this?" becomes "what were they trying to do, and what did
they approve?"

## The honest summary

Conversational platform operations are not about replacing the console. They are
about finally writing the runbook down in a form that can be executed, checked
and reviewed — with a human at the two or three points where a human genuinely
matters.

That is a smaller claim than most agent demos make. It is also the one that
survives contact with an enterprise change process.
